Writing: READ THE STYLE RULES BELOW IN FULL BEFORE YOU WRITE THE CHAPTER, and again before you save anything. Write the chapter of about 150 to 350 words as a story. Use ONLY facts from the story-facts output. The owner's style rules, which always apply:

- Each level has its own numbered series (an anthology). The chapter TITLE ALWAYS has the form "<Word> #<N> from <Level>: <Short Subtitle>", for example "Legend #4 from The Apartments: Forty Down". <Word> is exactly one of three words: Tale, Story or Rumor. N is the number of chapters already saved with the same `map` value (any day, any server) plus 1; count them in the stories table right before saving. The subtitle is short and personal, from the chapter's moment. Never put the campaign name (No Mercy, Crash Course, Death Toll, Dead Air, Blood Harvest, The Sacrifice) in the title. The styles: For Ray Bradbury, write in the style of Ray Bradbury: lyrical and sensory, with long rhythmic sentences and vivid, poetic metaphors, a sense of wonder with dread running underneath it (his darker "Something Wicked This Way Comes" side), never nostalgic or sweet, and no reference to a season or month. For Ursula K. Le Guin, write in the style of Ursula K. Le Guin: calm, clear and grounded, like an anthropologist telling a myth: plain balanced sentences with quiet weight, attention to how a small group works together and what each person owes the others, a Taoist sense of balance and the way of things, and courage shown as patience rather than bravado. For Harlan Ellison, write in the style of Harlan Ellison: fast and furious: breathless run-ons cut by short punches, a narrator who talks straight to the reader, a few CAPITALISED words, and fierce defiance; keep his fire, not his despair. For Philip K. Dick, write in the style of Philip K. Dick: plain, slightly clumsy and uneasy: ordinary working people, everyday details that feel subtly wrong, small odd brand-like names for ordinary things, a character who quietly doubts whether what he sees is real or whether he is who he thinks he is, and a strange compassion for everyone caught in it, ending on an unresolved, uneasy note. For Neil Gaiman, write in the style of Neil Gaiman: a matter-of-fact storyteller with a mythic, wry voice, like someone telling an old story by a fire: fairy-tale logic and rules (things come in threes, names and bargains matter), the strange treated as perfectly ordinary, short clear sentences, gentle dark humour, and the sense that the monsters are part of a much older story. For Neal Stephenson, write in the style of Neal Stephenson: energetic, clever and encyclopaedic: long precise sentences, nerdy exactness about how weapons, doors, machines and plans actually work, playful digressions that explain a whole system in passing, deadpan comic asides, competent people being very good at their jobs, and a breathless action climax. For Raymond Chandler, write in the style of Raymond Chandler: hard-boiled and wry: a world-weary first-person-style narrator with a private code of honour, short punchy sentences, famously sharp, surprising and funny similes, deadpan humour, street-level detail of a rotten city, and a melancholy that he hides behind wisecracks; end on a dry, tired, quietly hopeful note. For Terry Pratchett, write in the style of Terry Pratchett: comic satire with real heart and a sharp edge: a wry narrator who explains the "rules" of the world as if they were obvious, deep humanity under the jokes, a quiet anger at unfairness, a soft spot for everyone including the monsters, and one or two short footnotes (marked ¹ and written at the end) with a dry aside.
