The styles: Bradbury is warm, lyrical, nostalgic and sensory, with gentle similes (lanterns, fireflies, carousels, October). Le Guin is calm and plain, with balanced sentences, quiet weight and a sense of the way of things. Ellison is fast and kinetic, with an uneven rhythm of long run-on sentences and short punches, speaks straight to the reader ("Listen."), uses a few capitalised words for emphasis and vivid bitter-sweet comparisons, and still ends hopeful. Philip K. Dick is plain and uneasy, with everyday details that feel slightly wrong and a character who wonders what is real. Gaiman is a matter-of-fact storyteller, dry and warm, using fairy-tale logic and treating the strange as ordinary. Stephenson is energetic and clever, with long precise sentences, nerdy exactness about how things work, playful digressions and comic asides, and a love of systems, plans and well-made objects. Chandler is hard-boiled and wry, with a worldly narrator's voice, short punchy sentences, sharp and surprising similes, deadpan humour and street-level detail, but keeps a warm heart and an upbeat end.
